Opening

Protein is the one number you hit on purpose

Protein is the one number you hit on purpose. Everything else on your plate has room to move.

The Macros page covered why it matters. This page covers how you actually get it. Because actually hitting your protein goal on a Wednesday when kids are going crazy, you need to make dinner, and you don't have any meals prepped is where a lot of us would just order a pizza and say we will try again tomorrow. I will walk you through the exact steps I take to consistently hit my protein goal each day without failure.

Section 1

Your Target

Your protein goal gets set for you, and it does not move week to week.

I set your protein target before our kickoff call using your:

  • Height
  • Weight
  • Age
  • Activity level
  • And what your weight goals are.

When making adjustments to your numbers, this is one of the last things we move.

Two things people expect to be true here that are not:

Your target does not drop when your calories drop. In a deficit it matters more, not less, because a deficit is exactly when your body is most willing to break down muscle for energy.

Your target also does not go up because you had a hard training day. It is a daily number, not something you earn.

Hit the number every day. Don't tell yourself you will make it up the next day, that becomes a slippery slope.

Two Week Rule

Section 2

The 10x Rule

Cross out the last digit of the calorie number. If what's left lines up with the grams of protein, it's a real protein source.

This is the only math on this page and it works on any label, any package, any menu.

01

The shortcut

You don't need a calculator standing in the grocery aisle. Cross out the last digit of the calorie number and look at what's left. If that number lands close to the grams of protein, it's a real protein source. If it's way off, it isn't.

Nutrition Facts
Serving size 6 oz (170g)
Calories 200
Protein 30g

Label A: Chicken breast, 6 oz. Cross out the last zero of 200. You're left with 20. Line that up next to the protein: 30g. Close together, and the protein is even higher. This is a real protein source.

Nutrition Facts
Serving size 1 bar (60g)
Calories 300
Protein 10g

Label B: Protein bar, 1 bar. Cross out the last zero of 300. You're left with 30. Line that up next to the protein: 10g. Not close. This is a bar with a little protein riding along, not a protein source.

Illustrative labels

Crossing out the last digit is the same thing as dividing by ten, which is the whole point of the 10x rule. It's just fast enough to actually do while you're standing in the aisle instead of pulling out your phone.

02

The full math

If you want the exact number instead of the gut check, do the division. Calories divided by grams of protein.

Chicken breast is 185 calories and 35 grams of protein. 185 divided by 35 is 5. Peanut butter is 190 calories and 8 grams of protein. 190 divided by 8 is 24.

Both of those are food. Only one of them is a protein source.

ResultWhat it means
10 or underA real protein source. Build your meals out of these.
10 to 15Good food that carries some protein. It will not get you to your number on its own.
Over 15This is a fat or a carb with a little protein attached.

Cross out the last digit. If it lines up with the protein, it counts. RUN THE CHECK BEFORE YOU BUY IT.

03

What the check does not tell you

The check answers one question: is this an efficient way to buy protein. It does not tell you where the rest of the calories came from.

Two foods can both pass and spend your day very differently.

FoodServingCaloriesProteinFatCarbsCheck
Chicken breast, cooked4 oz18535 g4 g0 g5
90/10 ground beef, cooked4 oz20023 g11 g0 g9
Protein bar1 bar20020 g7 g20 g10

All three pass. The chicken gives you 35 grams and takes almost nothing out of your fat for the day. The beef gives you 23 grams and takes 11 grams of fat with it. The bar gives you 20 grams and takes both fat and carbs.

None of that makes the beef or the bar a bad choice. It means you have to know what else you just spent. So after the check passes, look at two more numbers on the same panel: the fat grams and the carb grams. If your fat is already close to where it needs to sit for the day, the chicken is the easier buy. If you have room, the beef tastes better and your family will actually eat it.

The check tells you a food is worth buying protein from. THE REST OF THE PANEL TELLS YOU WHAT ELSE YOU JUST BOUGHT.

Nothing is banned by this rule and it is not a good food and bad food list. It tells you which foods can carry your day and which ones cannot, and that is the difference between hitting your number and running out of calories at eight at night.

Section 3

What to Eat

These are the foods that do the work.

01

Chicken breast is king

Chicken breast is cheap, cooks in bulk, and clears the 10x rule with room to spare. It is good in the fridge for four to five days and takes on whatever seasoning you put on it, so eating it four days in a row doesn't have to taste like eating it four days in a row. This is why almost every default meal prep starts here.

If you get sick of it, lean ground beef, 93/7 or leaner, is the next thing I reach for. (I even cook 80/20, drain the grease out really well and log it as 93/7 sometimes. It is close enough.) The only issue with ground beef is that it costs more per pound and it carries more fat, so it takes a bigger bite out of your fat for the day. It also cooks in bulk just as easily and your family will actually eat it.

02

Build your meals around these

  • Chicken breast
  • Canned tuna
  • Shrimp
  • Egg whites
  • Whey protein
  • White fish, cod or tilapia
  • Deli turkey breast
  • Pork tenderloin
  • Nonfat Greek yogurt
  • Low fat cottage cheese
  • Lean ground beef, 93/7 or leaner
  • Firm tofu
  • Salmon
03

Protein pasta, protein oatmeal, and the new wave of protein products

Protein versions of pasta, oatmeal, bread, and cereal are everywhere now. Some of them are legitimate. A protein pasta can genuinely add real grams for close to the same calories as the regular version. Others add a scoop of whey to the ingredient list, print PROTEIN across the front in large letters, and barely add much more protein than the original "low protein" product.

Treat these exactly like anything else. Cross out the last digit, check it against the protein grams, then look at the fat and carbs underneath. Don't let the front of the package make the decision for you.

04

When the day got away from you

No cooking, no thinking, five minutes or less. I highly recommend keeping three of these in the house at all times, or picking it up on the way home on a busy night. If you can order a pizza, you can grab a rotisserie chicken from the grocery store.

  • Rotisserie chicken with the skin off
  • Tuna or salmon pouches
  • Nonfat Greek yogurt or cottage cheese
  • Deli turkey
  • Shrimp
  • Precooked chicken strips
  • Egg whites from a carton
  • A protein shake

The days you miss your protein are almost always the days you had nothing ready.

Section 4

Foods That Look Like Protein and Are Not

These get called protein foods constantly. Check the fat or carb content.

  • Whole eggs
  • Peanut butter
  • Cheddar cheese
  • Almonds and other nuts
  • Quinoa
  • Hummus
  • Trail mix

Peanut butter, nuts, and cheese trip people up the most, because all three get eaten by the handful and all three are thought of as protein sources to most people.

Whole eggs are the one that surprises people. A whole egg is a great food and a good fat source, not an efficient protein source. Most of the calories live in the yolk and the yolk carries very little of the protein. Two large eggs give you 13 grams of protein for 145 calories, which puts them at 11. That is not a knock on eggs. It means they can't anchor your protein plan the way egg whites or chicken can. This is why I usually cook two whole eggs with a weighed out portion of egg whites.

Pro tip: set the carton of egg whites on your food scale, zero the scale, and pour the amount you want into the pan, then set the carton back on the scale. The negative number left over is how much you used.

Quinoa is on the list for a different reason. It gets called a protein because it is a complete protein, which is true and beside the point. You would need four cups of it to reach a normal daily target, and that is 880 calories.

None of this is off limits. Eat the peanut butter if it fits your day. Just think of it as a fat, because if you are relying on these foods to get you to your number, you will run out of calories before you get there.

Section 5

How to Read a Label

Everything that matters is on the back of the package.

The front of a box is advertising. The nutrition label is regulated. Read the label.

01

Serving size, then servings per container

Start here every single time. Every number underneath describes one serving, not the package.

A common deceiving thing about nutrition labels is when the amount of food or drink in a package looks like a normal serving size, but the label lists more than one serving per package. I once drank a small bottle of chocolate milk thinking it was one serving at 220 calories, logged it in my app, and it came up as 220 calories for half the bottle. I checked the label again and it said two servings per bottle.

Everything else on the label is meaningless until you know what the serving is.
02

Calories and protein, in that order

Find both and run the 10x rule check before you leave the aisle.

03

Fat and carbs, right after

The 10x rule tells you the protein is worth buying. Fat and carb grams tell you what the rest of the calories are doing. Two seconds of reading, and it's the difference between a food that fits your day and a food that eats it.

04

Ignore the words on the front of the package

High protein is a legal technicality, not a nutritional one. The technicality is thin: a serving needs 10 grams of protein to print it on the front. Nothing governing the ratio of calories to protein. Nothing governing the ratio of protein to carbs and fat. Nothing governing the type or completeness of the protein added to reach the 10 grams.

A 300 calorie protein bar with 10 grams of protein meets the technicality that allows companies to label a food as "high protein." Run the 10x rule on it and it lands at 30, three times over the cutoff for a real protein source. A plain cup of cottage cheese, with no claim printed anywhere on the container, lands at 6.

Be very skeptical when you see the words "high protein" on a package. Think of it as marketing only. This is why whole foods like chicken, fish, beef, Greek yogurt, and cottage cheese are the reliable option for getting your protein in for the day.
05

Check what the serving is measured in

Grams and ounces are honest. Scoops, bars, pieces, and cups are where it gets soft. If a scoop is 32 grams and yours is heaping, you did not eat one scoop.

Go by the grams amount when weighing a serving, especially for powders. I have weighed a scoop of protein powder 8 grams short of what the scoop was supposed to be more than once.

06

Read the ingredients when the panel looks too good

The ingredient list runs in order by weight, heaviest first. If the first two or three ingredients are sugar, oil, or flour, that's most of what's in the package no matter what the front claims. Check it any time a food that sounds high protein is cheap, sweet, or shelf stable. Those three usually mean the food is carrying a lot of sugar or oil alongside the protein.

Section 6

Complete and Incomplete Protein

Animal protein covers all nine amino acids on its own. Plant protein can too, but usually takes more than one food across the day. Collagen never covers all nine amino acids.

Protein is not one substance. It is a chain of amino acids, and your body takes the chain apart and rebuilds it into whatever it needs. There are nine amino acids your body cannot make on its own, so they have to come in through food. A complete protein has all nine in useful amounts. An incomplete protein is short on one or more amino acids.

01

What's complete

Every animal source is complete. Meat, chicken, fish, eggs, dairy, whey, all of it. If you eat animal protein, you are not going to have this problem, and you do not need to think about this section again.

A few plant sources are complete on their own:

  • Soy, which covers tofu, edamame, tempeh, and soy milk
  • Quinoa
  • Buckwheat
  • Hemp seeds
02

What's incomplete, and why it usually doesn't matter

Most plants are missing at least one amino acid. Animal protein isn't, which is the whole reason to get nearly all your protein for the day from animal sources.

If you eat mostly or entirely plants, the fix is simple: eat a variety across the day and the gaps close on their own. Beans at lunch and rice at dinner does the job just as well as pairing them in the same meal.

03

The one that actually catches people

Collagen and gelatin do not count toward your protein target.

Collagen powder is protein by the label's definition, so the panel will show 10 or 20 grams and it will pass the 10x rule. But collagen is missing one of the nine entirely, and it is low in the one your body leans on hardest for muscle repair. Your body cannot use it to build or protect muscle the way it uses whey, chicken, or eggs.

If you like collagen for your skin, joints, or hair, take it. Just log it as something separate and do not let those grams stand in for your target. Twenty grams of collagen is not twenty grams of protein for our purposes.

If a protein source can't build muscle, it doesn't count toward the number I set for you. LOG IT, DON'T COUNT IT.
04

If you eat mostly or entirely plants

You can hit your number. It takes more attention and more calories.

  • Lean on the complete sources first. Soy is the most useful one on the list, because tofu, tempeh, and edamame all pass the 10x rule while beans and lentils land above 10.
  • Vary your plant sources across the week instead of eating the same one every day.
  • A pea or soy protein powder works exactly like whey does. If food alone doesn't get you to your number, use it to close the difference.
  • Expect the calories to run higher. Plant sources cost more calories per gram of protein, and that has to come out of somewhere. Tell me if this is your situation and we'll build the numbers around it from the start.
Section 7

How to Actually Hit Your Protein Goal

Build the day and each meal around protein first and the goal number takes care of itself.

The people who miss their target are almost never people who prepped each meal and planned out their day responsibly. It takes a choice to hit your protein goal. Make the choice the day before, and you will have no issues.

01

Anchor every meal

Pick the protein first, then build the rest of the plate around it. If you plan the meal and then look for somewhere to add protein, you have already used up your calories elsewhere.

02

Front load it

Making a real effort to get your protein at breakfast and lunch means dinner does not have to be a rescue mission chasing 70 grams at the end of the day.

My rule is to start the day with 30 grams of protein. Here's why: it starts muscle repair immediately, it keeps you full for hours instead of one, and it levels your blood sugar so the crash and the cravings never show up.

03

An example: how I do it, the Base Meal

Every four to five days I cook one to two six-packs of chicken breast and 5 cups of rice. I split it evenly across four meals for each day. This is what I call my Base Meal. It is what works for my schedule. It does not have to be four meals. Split it into three, five, whatever fits your day. What matters more than the number of meals is the spacing. I aim for 30 to 45 grams of protein every three to four hours, starting around 7am and finishing by 6pm.

Steady protein throughout the day keeps you full and is easier on your digestion than loading it all into one meal.

I always keep a backup in the pantry. Tuna packs, canned chicken, jerky. If life hits me in the face and meal prep doesn't happen, I still have something to grab on the way out the door.

04

Cooking for a family and sticking to your diet

You are not making yourself a separate dinner. Cook what everyone eats, keep the protein plain, and put the butter, cheese, and sauce on everyone else's plates instead of in the pan. Weigh your portion, serve theirs however they like it.

05

Stick to your Base Meal

One breakfast and one lunch you can grab and eat without thinking about it. You are not eating them every day forever. You are removing the chance of making a bad decision.

06

Weigh it, but don't chase the exact gram

Best practice is weighing food raw or dry, since that is the most accurate method and usually the number on the nutrition label. In real life, most people are weighing whatever's actually on the plate, which is usually cooked. Both are fine. The calorie tracking apps often have entries for most foods raw and cooked, so tracking is still fairly accurate.

What actually matters is not the exact number on any one day. It's the trend. If you eat roughly the same portion of your staple foods and log it roughly the same way meal to meal, the week to week weight trend tells us everything we need to know. If that trend isn't moving the way it should, that's when we adjust your number, not because one bowl of rice was a few grams off.

Protein is the only macro where hitting the number matters more than what you eat to hit it. GET THE GRAMS. Optimize the sources later.
Section 8

Where People Lose the Number

The same handful of mistakes account for most missed targets.

Leaning on nuts and cheese. Covered above. They are fat.

The food you eat standing up. The last four bites of a kid's dinner, the crust off their plate, a handful of cereal at the counter, the spoon you licked. None of it feels like eating. All of it counts, and almost none of it is protein. In a house with kids this is the largest untracked source of calories there is.

Counting collagen toward the target. Covered in Section 6. It passes the 10x rule but it does not do the job.

Skipping breakfast and never catching up. If you would rather eat later in the day, that is fine. The protein still has to fit in a smaller window. The number does not shrink because your eating window did.

Undercounting shrinkage. Meat loses roughly a quarter of its weight in cooking. Eight ounces raw is not eight ounces on the plate. This matters most if you're switching weighing methods back and forth on the same food. Weigh raw when it's easy, weigh cooked when it's not, and let the weight trend catch anything that drifts. I personally just weigh it after it is cooked because I cook in bulk.

Trusting the weight on the package. A chicken breast labeled six ounces is rarely six ounces. Weigh it. One scoop of protein powder could be 36 grams, or it could be 28 grams. Weigh it.

Section 9

Powders and Shakes

A protein shake is a totally acceptable source of protein.

Whey is dried dairy protein. It is convenient, it digests easily, and it is one of the most efficient ways to close a gap at the end of the day. It does not do anything a chicken breast does not do. It is just faster and it fits in a bag.

Nothing about it is required. If you can hit your number with food, hit it with food. Most people cannot, most days, and a shake is the difference between hitting the target and missing it by twenty grams four times a week.

A shake is a tool for closing a gap. It is not the plan.

If you are choosing one, cross out the last digit on the label and ignore everything else on the front. A good powder passes the 10x rule. If it comes out way off, you are buying a meal replacement, not a protein powder.

If dairy does not sit well with you, an isolate or a plant blend does the same job.

Closing

What to Do With This

Protein is the least complicated thing you will do and it carries the most value next to carbs. It protects the muscle you have while you lose fat, it keeps you full when your calories are tight, and it is linked to better bone density over the long run.

You do not need a crazy strategy for it. You need your number, a short list of foods that pass the 10x rule, and something ready in the fridge for the days that go sideways.

If you haven't eaten this much protein consistently before, expect some bloating or gas the first couple weeks. That's your gut adjusting, not a sign anything is wrong. A digestive enzyme supplement, specifically one with protease, can ease that adjustment while your body catches up.
